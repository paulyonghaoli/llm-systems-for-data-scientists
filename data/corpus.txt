A tokenizer is a compression scheme that has been frozen into a contract. It is
fitted once, on a corpus chosen by someone else, and then it never changes
again for the life of the model. Everything the model reads passes through it,
and everything the model writes comes back out through it. If the tokenizer
handles some kind of text badly, the model handles that text badly, and there
is no amount of prompting that repairs the damage.

This is an unfamiliar shape for anyone who arrives from ordinary machine
learning. In a normal pipeline the vectorizer belongs to you. You fit it on
your training split, you inspect the vocabulary, you notice that it has
learned three spellings of the same word, and you fix the preprocessing. The
vectorizer is a knob. Here it is a wall. You did not fit it, you cannot refit
it, and in most cases you cannot even see the corpus it was fitted on.

The consequences are practical rather than philosophical. Text is billed by
the token, not by the word and not by the character, so the cost of a request
depends on a function you did not choose. Context limits are stated in tokens,
so how much of a document fits depends on the same function. A budget computed
in words will be wrong, and it will be wrong by a different factor for every
language and every kind of document you feed it.

Consider what happens with numbers. A run of digits is not one symbol to a
tokenizer. Depending on how the vocabulary was built, the year may be one
token, or two, or four. Two numbers that a person reads as obviously similar
can arrive at the model with completely different shapes. This is one of the
reasons that arithmetic is harder for these systems than their other abilities
suggest. The difficulty is partly in the input representation, before any
reasoning has been attempted at all.

Whitespace behaves in a way that surprises people the first time they measure
it. In most modern vocabularies the leading space belongs to the word that
follows it. The word at the start of a sentence and the same word in the
middle of a sentence are therefore different tokens, with different
identifiers and different learned representations. Strip the spaces from a
prompt to save room and you have not saved room; you have replaced every
common token with a rarer one and made the input stranger than it was.

Languages are not billed equally. A vocabulary fitted mostly on English will
represent common English words as single tokens and will fall back to raw
bytes for scripts it has not seen. Because those scripts often use three bytes
per character where English uses one, the same sentence can cost several times
as much to send. The same document therefore has several different lengths
depending only on the language it happens to be written in, and any system
that charges by the token charges accordingly.

Code is its own case. Indentation is a large fraction of a source file, and
whether a run of four spaces is one token or four is a decision made long
before you arrived. Structured data such as JSON is worse: the punctuation is
repetitive, the keys repeat on every record, and a format designed to be
convenient for programs turns out to be an expensive way to speak to a model.
The same information expressed as a compact table can cost a fraction of what
it costs as a list of objects.

None of this is visible from the outside. A request that overruns a context
limit does not usually announce itself as a tokenization problem. It arrives
as a truncated document, a missing final instruction, an answer that ignores
the last half of the input. The failure looks like a reasoning failure and it
is really an accounting failure. This is why measuring token counts directly,
on your own real documents, is worth more than any rule of thumb about words
per token.

The algorithm behind most of these vocabularies is simple enough to write in
an afternoon. Start with the raw bytes, because starting with bytes means
nothing can ever be out of vocabulary. Count how often each adjacent pair of
symbols occurs. Take the most frequent pair, give it a new symbol, and replace
every occurrence. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the size you asked for.
That is the whole method. What makes one implementation different from
another is not the loop but the decisions around it.

The first decision is where merges are not allowed to happen. Without a rule,
the procedure will cheerfully learn a single symbol for a full stop followed
by a space followed by the word that usually starts the next sentence. Such a
symbol is frequent and therefore attractive, and it is also useless, because
it welds together things that have nothing to do with each other. So the text
is cut into pieces first, along boundaries between letters, digits,
punctuation and whitespace, and merges are confined within a piece.

The second decision is what to do about ties. Two pairs will often occur
exactly as often as each other, especially early in training on a small
corpus. Any rule for choosing between them works. Having no rule does not
work, because the vocabulary then depends on the order a dictionary happened
to be built in, and two runs of the same program on the same text produce
different tokenizers. Reproducibility here is not a nicety; a vocabulary that
cannot be regenerated cannot be audited.

The third decision is when to stop. Asking for a vocabulary of a given size
does not guarantee you receive one. Once no pair occurs more than once, every
further merge spends a vocabulary slot to shorten exactly one position in the
corpus, which is not a trade worth making. A small corpus therefore yields a
small vocabulary no matter what size was requested, and a training run that
quietly returns fewer symbols than asked for is behaving correctly.

Scale is the part that cannot be reproduced on a laptop. A vocabulary fitted
on a few kilobytes of one person's prose learns that particular person's
habits. A vocabulary fitted on a large fraction of the written internet learns
the shapes that recur across many languages and many genres. The algorithm is
identical. The difference in outcome is entirely a difference in the data, and
it shows up most clearly in the text furthest from the training distribution,
where the small vocabulary degrades all the way back to individual bytes while
the large one still has something useful to say.

Measurement is the habit worth keeping. Take your own documents, count the
tokens, divide by the characters, and look at the number. Do it separately for
prose, for code, for structured data and for every language you actually
serve. The result is a small table that will predict your costs and your
truncation failures better than any general advice, and it takes a few minutes
to produce. Whatever else changes about these systems, that table stays
useful, because it describes your data rather than somebody else's model.
