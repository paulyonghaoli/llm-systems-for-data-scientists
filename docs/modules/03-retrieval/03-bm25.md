# 3.3 · BM25, and why the old baseline still wins

## A · Why this matters

Lexical retrieval is the thing every embedding demo implicitly claims to have
replaced, and on this corpus it answers
22 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.lexical_only --> queries that dense retrieval
misses entirely. It needs no model, no GPU, no fixture, no index rebuild when
the model version changes, and it runs in milliseconds on a corpus this size.
Skipping it is the most expensive mistake available in this module.

The number that should decide how seriously you take this: BM25 reaches
recall@10 of 0.551 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.recall_plain --> against dense
retrieval's 0.614 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.dense_recall -->, so the
learned model wins — and *either one finding the answer* reaches
0.739 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.union_ceiling -->. There are
12.5 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.headroom_pts --> points sitting between the
better single retriever and what the two of them already know between them,
which is more than any tuning in this module recovers.

There is also a cheaper lesson here about the implementation everybody writes
first. The plain textbook BM25 — the one in this repository's
`tools/verify_corpus.py`, with no stopword handling — scores **zero** on the
multi-hop queries. Not "poorly". Zero. Removing common terms takes those
queries to 1.00 and the overall figure to
0.705 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.recall_stopworded -->, a gain of
15.4 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.stopword_gain_pts --> points from deleting
108 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.n_stopwords --> words.

!!! info "Terms used in this lesson"
    **Term frequency (`tf`)** — how many times a term occurs in a document.
    More occurrences mean more relevance, but with sharply diminishing returns.

    **Document frequency (`df`)** — how many documents contain a term at all.
    High document frequency means the term discriminates poorly.

    **Inverse document frequency (`idf`)** — the weight BM25 gives a term,
    large for rare terms and near zero for ubiquitous ones.

    **`k1`** — the saturation parameter. It sets how quickly repeated
    occurrences of a term stop adding score. Typical values are 1.2 to 2.0.

    **`b`** — the length-normalisation parameter, from 0 (ignore document
    length entirely) to 1 (normalise fully by it).

    **Stopword** — a term so common it carries no retrieval signal. Here they
    are *derived from the corpus* rather than imported: any term in more than
    35% <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.stopword_df_threshold_pct -->% of
    documents, which in this domain includes "consignment".

## B · Mental model

**BM25 scores a document by how surprising its overlap with the query is,
discounted by how much room the document had to contain that overlap by
chance.**

Every part of the formula is one of those two ideas. Rare terms score more than
common ones, because sharing a rare term is surprising and sharing "the" is
not. Repeated terms score more, but with saturation, because the fifth
occurrence of a word tells you much less than the first. Long documents score
less for the same match, because a long document contains more words and is
therefore more likely to contain yours accidentally.

The failure this produces is the one the experiment exposes. When a query is
mostly common words, the rare terms in it contribute a small share of the
total, and the length-normalisation term decides the ranking instead — so the
shortest documents win regardless of what they say. In this corpus the shortest
documents are the FAQs, and a multi-hop query like *"which bay would
consignment TL-3863 be staged in if it were flagged"* retrieves the same five
FAQs no matter which consignment you ask about. The identifier, which is the
only informative token in the query, is outvoted.

??? question "If the identifier `TL-3863` appears in exactly one document, why doesn't its enormous idf dominate?"
    It has a large weight, but it is one term among thirteen, and the other
    twelve all match the short FAQs while contributing length-normalised
    scores of their own. BM25 sums over query terms, so a single strong signal
    can be outweighed by many weak ones — which is exactly what stopword
    removal fixes, by deleting the weak ones rather than by boosting the
    strong one.

## C · Mechanism

The score of document `D` for query `Q` is a sum over the query's terms:

$$
\text{score}(D, Q) = \sum_{t \in Q} \text{idf}(t) \cdot
\frac{f(t, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(t, D) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
$$

with the Robertson inverse document frequency

$$
\text{idf}(t) = \ln\left(1 + \frac{N - n(t) + 0.5}{n(t) + 0.5}\right)
$$

where `N` is the collection size and `n(t)` the number of documents containing
`t`. Three behaviours follow directly, and each is worth checking against the
formula rather than taking on trust.

**Saturation.** As `f` grows, the fraction approaches `k1 + 1`, so the score
contributed by any single term is bounded. A document mentioning a term fifty
times cannot outrank one mentioning it five times by a factor of ten. Setting
`k1` low makes the bound bite sooner; at `k1 = 0` the term frequency stops
mattering at all and BM25 degenerates to a presence-weighted idf sum.

**Length normalisation.** The `b` term scales `k1` by the document's length
relative to the collection average. At `b = 0` length is ignored entirely; at
`b = 1` a document twice the average length needs roughly twice the term
frequency for the same score. This is the parameter that decides the FAQ
problem above.

**Rarity.** The idf formula gives a term appearing in one document out of
2,419 a weight around 7.4, and a term in half of them a weight around 1.1.
That ratio is the entire discriminating power of the method, and it collapses
when a term is common — which is why deleting common terms costs nothing and
gains a great deal.

Stopword removal is not part of BM25 and is nearly always necessary. The
version in `tools/verify_corpus.py` deliberately omits it, because gate 25 uses
it as a *plain* lexical baseline and a tuned retriever would make "BM25 fails
here" a statement about the tuning. That decision is defensible for the gate
and would be wrong for a system.

??? question "Why does setting `k1` to zero turn BM25 into something that ignores term frequency entirely?"
    Put `k1 = 0` into the fraction and the denominator becomes `f`, so the
    whole term collapses to `idf(t) * f * 1 / f`, which is just `idf(t)`. Every
    document containing the term scores the same for it, however many times it
    appears. That is the presence-weighted idf sum BM25 generalises, and it is
    a reminder that `k1` interpolates between two named models rather than
    being an arbitrary dial.

## D · From data science to LLM systems

BM25 is a bag-of-words model with hand-derived feature weights, and you have
built its cousin: TF-IDF plus cosine similarity, which lesson 2.2 already used
for few-shot selection. The differences are that BM25 saturates term frequency
instead of taking it linearly, and normalises by document length explicitly
rather than through vector normalisation. If you have ever watched a TF-IDF
model get dominated by a long document that mentioned everything, you have met
the problem `b` exists to solve.

The analogy breaks in three places worth knowing.

**There is no training set, so there is nothing to overfit and nothing to
validate against.** `k1` and `b` are chosen, not learned, and the sweep in §G
shows they matter — which means the honest workflow is to tune them against a
labelled query set exactly as you would a hyperparameter, and most teams
instead accept whatever the library defaults to.

**Vocabulary is the model.** A term absent from the corpus has no idf and
contributes nothing, so a query using words nobody wrote retrieves nothing,
silently. A dense retriever degrades gracefully here and BM25 does not degrade
at all — it simply returns whatever matched the remaining words, which is the
mechanism behind its 0.03 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.plain_vocabulary_mismatch -->
on the vocabulary-mismatch queries.

**Stopwords are domain-specific, and importing a list is a bug waiting to
happen.** In this corpus "consignment" appears in most documents and carries no
information; in a general collection it is a rare and highly discriminating
term. Deriving the list from document frequency adapts automatically. Importing
an English stopword list would leave "consignment" in and take out words that
might have mattered.

## E · Minimal implementation

The scoring loop, with the postings list that makes it fast:

```python
def rank(self, query, limit=10):
    scores = defaultdict(float)
    for w in tokenize(query):
        if w in self.stop:
            continue
        idf = self.idf.get(w)
        if idf is None:      # not in the corpus at all: contributes nothing
            continue
        for i in self.postings[w]:
            f = self.tf[i][w]
            denom = f + self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * self.lens[i] / self.avg)
            scores[i] += idf * f * (self.k1 + 1) / denom
    top = sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda kv: (-kv[1], kv[0]))[:limit]
    return [self.ids[i] for i, _ in top]
```

The line that matters for performance is `for i in self.postings[w]` — the loop
runs over documents *containing the term*, not over the corpus. That is what
makes lexical search cheap where dense search is not: a rare term touches a
handful of documents, so a specific query costs almost nothing, and only a
query of common words is expensive. Dense retrieval has the opposite profile,
paying the same full scan whatever you ask.

Worth making concrete, because it is the reason lexical search survives in
systems that have embeddings available. A query for a consignment identifier
touches the single document containing it, so the scoring loop runs once. A
query for "the depot" touches every document containing either word, which here
is essentially the whole corpus — 2,419 iterations for a query that will not
discriminate anyway. The expensive queries and the useless queries are the same
queries, which means stopword removal buys latency and quality from one change.

The tie-break in the sort is not cosmetic. Ranking by score alone leaves
documents with identical scores in dictionary order, which varies between runs
under different insertion orders; adding the index makes the ranking
deterministic and the tests reproducible.

??? question "Your corpus is one million short product titles of nearly identical length. Which parameter stops mattering?"
    `b`, almost entirely. Length normalisation divides by each document's
    length relative to the average, so when every document is the same length
    the correction is the same constant everywhere and cannot reorder
    anything. That is the opposite of this corpus, where lengths span 141 to
    354 tokens between the tenth and ninetieth percentiles and `b` moves recall
    by thirteen points.

## F · Production practice

**Derive stopwords from your corpus, and store the list with the index.** A
document-frequency threshold adapts to the domain automatically. Recompute it
when the corpus composition changes, and version it alongside the index, for
the same reason lesson 3.1's mean and component are versioned — a query
filtered against a different stopword list than the documents were is a silent
mismatch.

**Tune `k1` and `b` against labelled queries.** Measured below, `b` moves
recall by more than thirteen points across its range on this corpus, and the
best value is not the default at either end.

**Keep the lexical index even after you add embeddings.** It answers 22 queries
here that dense retrieval does not, it needs no re-embedding when the model
changes, and it is the only part of the system that still works when the
embedding service is down.

**Do not import an English stopword list into a specialised corpus.** It will
retain the domain terms that carry no signal and delete general words that do.

??? question "You add 50,000 documents from a new product line. What has to be recomputed, and what breaks if you skip it?"
    Document frequencies, and therefore both the idf table and the derived
    stopword list. Skipping it leaves terms weighted by how rare they were in
    the old corpus — a term that has just become common keeps a high idf and
    starts dominating rankings it should no longer influence. Nothing raises,
    and the symptom is that recently-added documents retrieve strangely.

| lexical_distractor | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| multi_hop | **0.00** | **1.00** |

The overall gain is 31 queries won against

**Multi-hop goes from zero to one.** Nothing about those queries changed except
that eleven common words stopped being scored, and that was the entire
difference between never finding the document and always finding it.

**Near-duplicates get worse, and that is not noise.** Six depot handbooks are
roughly ninety per cent identical, and part of what distinguished them was
their common vocabulary. Deleting it deleted the distinction. A single
"improvement" that moves one query class from 0.00 to 1.00 and another from
0.37 to 0.23 is why this table is a distribution rather than an average.

**Parameters.** Document lengths here span
141 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.doc_len_p10 --> to
354 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.doc_len_p90 --> tokens between the tenth and
ninetieth percentiles, so `b` has something to act on.

| `b` | 0.0 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 1.0 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| recall@10 | 0.653 | **0.574** | 0.665 | **0.705** | 0.699 |

That curve is not monotonic, and the dip is real rather than sampling noise:
against `b = 0.25`, setting `b = 0.75` wins
31 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.won_b --> queries and loses
8 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.lost_b -->, at
p = 0.0003 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.p_b -->. Partial length normalisation
is worse here than either ignoring length or normalising fully, which no
description of the parameter would lead you to predict, and which is a decent
argument for sweeping it rather than reasoning about it.

`k1` matters much less on this corpus — 0.716 at `k1 = 0.5` down to
0.676 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.recall_k1_40 --> at `k1 = 4.0` — because
these documents rarely repeat a term often enough for saturation to bind.

**Complementarity with dense retrieval.** Of 176 answerable queries:

| | count |
|---|---:|
| both find it | 75 |
| lexical only | 22 |
| dense only | 33 |
| neither | 46 |

BM25 alone is 0.551, dense alone is 0.614, and either-one is
0.739 <!-- computed: bm25_behaviour.union_ceiling -->. The 22 lexical-only
queries are the ones where an exact identifier or a legal phrase appears
verbatim; the 33 dense-only are paraphrases. Neither method is a subset of the
other, which is the precondition for lesson 3.4 to have anything to combine.

??? question "If stopword removal helped this much, why not raise the threshold further and delete more?"
    Because the near-duplicate row is already going the wrong way at 35%, and
    every increase deletes more of what distinguishes documents from each
    other. The threshold is a tradeoff between deleting noise and deleting
    signal, and where it sits depends on how similar your documents are — which
    means it is another parameter to sweep against labelled queries rather than
    to set from a rule of thumb.

## H · Failure modes and cost traps

**Shipping textbook BM25.** It has no stopword handling, and this corpus shows
it scoring zero on an entire query class as a result. The formula in a paper is
not a retrieval system.

**Importing a general stopword list.** Domain terms that carry no signal stay
in, and general words that might have discriminated go out. Derive it from
document frequency.

**Assuming stopword removal is free.** Measured here it costs near-duplicate
queries 14 points. Anything that removes information removes some you wanted.

**Leaving `k1` and `b` at their defaults on an unusual corpus.** The default
`b = 0.75` happens to be right here, which you can only know by sweeping it,
and the neighbouring value is thirteen points worse.

**Treating a missing term as a weak match.** A query term absent from the
corpus contributes exactly nothing and raises nothing. A query composed
entirely of such terms returns whatever matched the remaining words, ranked
confidently.

**Retiring the lexical index when embeddings arrive.** It answers a fifth of
these queries that the embedding model cannot, and it is the component that
keeps working when the model or its service does not.

**A mistake this lesson found in this repository.** Investigating the zero on
multi-hop showed the same five FAQs returned for every such query regardless of
the consignment id in it — the identifier was contributing nothing to the
ranking. That prompted a check of whether gate 25, which uses this plain BM25
to assert that lexical retrieval fails on vocabulary mismatch, was measuring a
strawman. Re-run against the stopword-removing version, gate 25's checks hold
at 97% and 100% against their 50% thresholds, so the planted phenomena survive
a much stronger baseline. Worth checking rather than assuming: a gate whose
baseline is weak proves less than it appears to.

## J · Annotated references

- **Robertson and Zaragoza (2009), "The Probabilistic Relevance Framework:
  BM25 and Beyond"** — the authoritative account by the people who built it.
  §3 derives the saturation and length-normalisation terms rather than
  asserting them.
- **Trotman, Puurula and Burgess (2014), "Improvements to BM25 and Language
  Models Examined"** — an empirical sweep of the variants and parameters
  across collections. Useful calibration for how much `k1` and `b` move
  depending on the corpus.
- **Thakur et al. (2021), "BEIR"** — the benchmark where BM25 embarrassed a
  generation of dense retrievers on out-of-domain tasks. The relevant result
  is not that lexical wins, but that it wins where the training distribution
  ends.
- **Lin, Nogueira and Yates (2021), "Pretrained Transformers for Text
  Ranking"** — chapter 3 for the lexical baselines a neural system has to beat
  before it is worth deploying.

## K · Extension

*Off-platform, an hour or two.* Take your own corpus and reproduce the stopword
sweep: compute document frequency, vary the threshold from 20% to 60%, and plot
recall by query type rather than in aggregate. The result to look for is
whether any query class moves in the opposite direction from the average, as
near-duplicates do here. If none does, your query set is probably not diverse
enough to be telling you the truth about the system.
